What we found (BER)

Short answer
Teams that get out of their own end well do win more, but breaking out is hard to tell apart from simply out-shooting the opponent. The best score built only from a team's own breakouts predicts games it has not seen at +0.40; the best 'breakout battle' score (the team's numbers minus the opponent's) reaches +0.46, level with shot-attempt share (Corsi, +0.46). After shot share and shot quality are taken out, what is left is +0.09 with a noise margin of 0.08. These numbers come from the seasons used to search; the blind test on five kept-back seasons is the final word.
The blind test
On five seasons that no test had touched (2009-10 to 2013-14), the own-breakouts recipe predicted unseen games at +0.40 and the breakout-battle recipe at +0.38, against +0.45 for Corsi on the same seasons. Neither reached the 0.46 line set beforehand. After shot share and shot quality were taken out, what was left was +0.06 and +0.02: no separate edge. Both links were positive in all five seasons, so the plain finding stands: teams that break out well do win more, but the breakout scores are not a better measure of a team than its shot share.
The best single stats
The best single stat is pinned-in spells: it predicts games it has not seen at +0.38. BER, the best stat that is not built from shot counts, is at +0.32. Shot-attempt share (Corsi) is at +0.46.
What helped
Counting each stat as a battle, the team's number minus the opponent's in the same games, helped: the average stat predicts unseen games at +0.25 as a battle against +0.20 on its own. Counting only moments when the score is close did not help (+0.18).
Is it more than out-shooting the opponent?
Not clearly. After shot share and shot quality are taken out, the breakout-battle recipe leaves +0.09, about the size of its noise margin (0.08), and the own-breakouts recipe leaves +0.02. The blind test, on fresh seasons, found nothing clear either.
Does it help pick contenders?
Not so far. Each of the first 8 breakout stats was added to the contender score and tried on seasons the score had not seen: 0 of 8 improved it by more than the noise. BER and the two recipes have not been put through that test yet.

Two recipes

Every combination of up to four of these stats was tried (about 867,000 of them), each also counted in close-score moments only and as a battle against the opponent. To avoid keeping a lucky winner, the best combination was always chosen without the season it was scored on. "Own breakouts" uses only the team's own numbers; "breakout battle" also uses what the team does to its opponents, so it measures the forecheck as well as the breakout.

How BER is read from the play-by-play

BER (breakout efficiency rating) is 100 × (controlled exits + half the safe clears) ÷ breakout attempts. The play-by-play does not record who has the puck, so each breakout attempt is judged by the first telling event in the 20 seconds after it:

  • Breakout attempt: the team wins the puck in its own zone: a faceoff win, a takeaway, a blocked shot, or the opponent missing the net.
  • Controlled exit: the next telling event is beyond the blue line and shows the team still had the puck: its own shot attempt, its own giveaway, a hit on one of its players, or the puck being taken off it there.
  • Safe clear: the next telling event is beyond the blue line but the opponent has the puck, and it does not come straight back.
  • Failed: a shot attempt against, a turnover in the zone, or an icing comes first.
  • Cannot be judged: nothing telling happens in time. These attempts are left out. About 4 in 10 attempts end up here.

In 2025-26, of the attempts that could be judged, 23% were controlled exits, 11% safe clears and 66% failed.

How BEI is built

BEI (breakout efficiency index, version 1) blends three numbers. Each is turned into a league percentile within the season (100 for the best team, 0 for the worst) and then weighted:

  • 45%: own-zone giveaways per hour of own-zone time (fewer is better). Giveaways in the team's own zone at 5-on-5, from the play-by-play, divided by the hours of 5-on-5 play the team spent in its own zone.
  • 30%: share of time spent in the own zone (less is better), at even strength, from the NHL's EDGE puck and player tracking.
  • 25%: transition shot attempts per 60 minutes (more is better) at 5-on-5: a shot attempt within 5 seconds of play coming up the ice.

NHL EDGE zone time starts in 2021-22, so BEI does too. The weights were set by hand; they were not fitted to the data or tested on seasons they were not set on.

Giving more weight to own-zone time would make BEI line up better with winning, but most of that is the same information as shot share.

About this page

The question: Do teams that get out of their own end cleanly win more? And is that anything more than out-shooting the opponent?

Built from the NHL's play-by-play at 5-on-5: every faceoff, shot, hit, giveaway and takeaway, with where on the ice it happened. Ten regular seasons, 2014-15 to 2025-26 (2019-20 and 2020-21 are left out as unusual seasons). Every number in the tables is a correlation worked out on the teams' rankings within each season: 0 means no relationship, 1 a perfect one. Terms are explained on the About page. The Corsi and expected-goals yardsticks and 5-on-5 ice time are from MoneyPuck.com, used here for non-commercial purposes with credit.

How it was tested, and the limits

  • These are event counts, not puck tracking. A clean exit often leaves no event behind, while a shot against is always recorded, so failures are over-counted. The level of BER here cannot be compared with hand-tracked numbers; only the ranking of teams means something.
  • A weak result can mean breakouts matter less than believed, or that events cannot see breakouts well enough. This page cannot tell those apart.
  • A link is not proof of cause: good teams may break out well because they are good, not the other way round.
  • These are season-level results about how strong a team is. They say nothing about who wins a single game.
  • With this many combinations tried, the numbers from the ten search seasons are flattered a little by the search. The blind test above is the fair judge.
  • BEI is a first version: its weights were set by hand and it has not been through the hold-out or blind tests that BER went through. Its transition count is this site's own play-by-play version, so it will not match tracking-based transition numbers published elsewhere.
